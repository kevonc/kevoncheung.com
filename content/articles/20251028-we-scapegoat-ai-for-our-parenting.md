A friend sent me this video and asked me for my opinion. I had to tell him, “Ok, wow. I need some time to digest what this means and get back to you!”

![A little girl bids farewell to her AI robot](/images/articles/scapegoat-ai-video.png)

There’s this little girl from China, about 6 or 7 years old. It seems like her AI gadget is broken, so she’s saying goodbye. The AI has this super soft and sentimental voice, saying words like “Can I teach you one last word? Memory. I will always remember the happy times with you.” “I will miss you a lot, too.” “Keep being the kid who loves to ask questions and learn!”

And my reaction started going on a roller coaster ride.

I first thought, “Oh my, this is so sad.” The fact that a human being and a verbal-only AI can develop such a bond is disturbing. What will humanity become soon?

Then I got to “How much time is this little girl spending with this AI?” Kids get feelings easily. Something is very off here, but I don’t know what yet.

Finally, I landed on “What does her day look like? Who is spending time with her?” I know that people are spending less time with one another these days. I also know parents who barely spend time with their kids. Is this AI her absolute best buddy on earth?

I started comparing notes with my experience introducing AI to my daughter, Avery. I even built a speaking practice tool, Chatty Bestie, to use with her. I’m not afraid of introducing AI to my daughters, but why is this video still somewhat disturbing?

I had to find out why. And so I started reflecting:

- Why don’t I see addictive behavior from my daughter?
- What’s the difference in experience between this Chinese girl and my daughter?
- What does this all mean to us?

Then it all made sense to me.

## Are we delegating humanity to AI?

It’s actually not just kids who form emotional attachments easily. If I have a jacket that I’ve worn for years, I develop an attachment to it and don’t want to throw it out.

Of course, when kids spend a lot of time with something, they’re going to get attached. Be it a plush toy, a dog, a cat, a blanket, a pillow, or anything else.

In the video, I had this disturbing feeling (and hypothesis) that the parents are delegating their job of companionship to AI.

Maybe they’re too busy or lazy to spend time with their kids. This is actually very common these days.

That’s exactly where everything can go wrong.

I have two young children (under 5), and there is no way we’d give AI to a kid as a companion.

Because honestly, that’s our job as parents.

Say I really have no choice and have to delegate companionship to an AI (as in they’re chatting freely and AI is the emotional support). How much time is okay?

Let’s say 10 mins a day with AI, that’s 0.7% of a day. Not bad. My daughters are easily spending way more time with real human beings than that.

But if my daughters are spending 30 mins a day with AI, that’s 2.1% of a day. I’d say it’s alarming. That’s too much time free-talking with AI.

The rule of thumb is that I won’t let kids spend more time with AI than with human beings.

The fact is, AI is not inherently a bad thing. It is a tool that can help us. I see it like a knife: It can be used to cut food, amazing. But it can also be used as a murder weapon, horrible.

If you leave a young child in a room with a knife without explaining what it does, what do you imagine would happen? So a lot of the AI education that we parents need to do is on preparing our kids about what the right thing and wrong thing to do with this tool.

Since we cannot get rid of this “knife” from the world already, this is something that we must get right. Yes, urgently, before your kids have any access to AI.

## Nail a specific use case

If AI is a tool like a knife, then we also need to be clear about what this tool is for.

So far, I see that a great use case is to improve a kid’s verbal communication.

When I asked my daughter, Avery, to practice English (her third language) with me, she always resisted. She felt weird speaking a different language to me. She got the feeling of “practice” and didn’t like it.

As parents, it is hard not to feel like the “bad guys” in learning sometimes.

That’s why I built Chatty Bestie.

When I sit down with Avery to chat with Chatty Bestie, she is much more interested and excited. She talks freely without the pressure of “daddy is trying to quiz me in this third language.” I sit next to her, cheering her on and sharing tiny tips here and there. Instead of being “the teacher,” I’m now her teammate on this quest.

This change of learning dynamic is what surprised me the most. I start to see how AI can become a key part in this triangular relationship to help our kids learn.

But we also need to be careful when introducing AI as a learning buddy. Here are my conditions:

- Parents are as present as possible, which is a must for young children under 8
- Parents can follow the entire interaction transparently
- Parents introduce this AI as a robot to help with a specific goal, not a general companion

Lydia and I spend a lot of time thinking and reflecting on this, so I think we’re managing okay so far. For parents who are not educators, I can see that it is extremely difficult to draw a fine line.

Other than verbal communication, I’ve come across drawing and storytelling to be two use cases where AI can be a great learning buddy.

But here’s the thing: even with these helpful tools for chatting, drawing, or spinning stories, it all falls apart if we’re not right there guiding things. The real problem isn’t the AI. It’s us parents slacking off and handing over too much control.

## The real danger is actually us (parents)

The biggest danger we face now is digital under-parenting.

I first learned about this term from Bill Gates’ note on the book, The Anxious Generation. He described “digital under-parenting” as giving kids unlimited and unsupervised access to devices and social media.

Overall, I think modern parents have gone soft on this. They like to:

- Reward with screen time
- Abandon supervision

I was at a workshop on “Raising Kids in this Digital Age” by Mila Devenport last month.

![Mila Devenport presenting how a phone-based childhood happened](/images/articles/scapegoat-ai-childhood.jpg)

It was a fantastic workshop. I especially loved how she fostered an open discussion among parents and educators (often both!).

A few parents shared that they reward their kids (10-year-olds and up) with screen time. One mom said, “It’s the only thing that can motivate my boy. I need it as a secret weapon.”

From the first sound of it, this line sounded so bad, right?

Yes, but it’s a lot more nuanced than it sounds.

I’ve been learning a lot about incentivizing motivation for short-term habits. This long article has highlighted some too. I’ve come to support that it is okay to do so because once we can shape a kid’s habits, we can keep that habit going even when the incentive is gone.

But when “incentivizing motivation for short-term habits” becomes “reward game time so that my boy does his homework,” it changes the entire meaning.

One, doing homework should be the kid’s responsibility. I think it is okay to unlock game time when homework is done, but it is not okay to reward “more” game time for basic responsibility.

Then, if this incentive system triggers gaming addiction, it is no longer about habit shaping. It becomes intolerable.

As for supervision, it sounds to me like a lot of times parents have zero idea what their kids are doing on devices. What games are they playing? Why must they play at 8pm tonight?

I don’t have sons or adolescent kids yet, so I don’t think I’ll be able to fully understand. My version of supervision is more on understanding their hobbies and interests, taking an interest in what they do, and maybe even taking part to help them achieve it.

To me, screen time is not bad. It only becomes bad when parents lose control over it and are withholding it like a magical candy.

## Be the one to leverage AI

Back to the little girl in the video breaking down to bid farewell to her AI gadget, my best guess is that she treats the AI as her best companion.

She spends a tremendous amount of time with it.

The AI cares for her more than anyone.

These are the things we parents have to be careful of. If we’re able to be there for our kids, to spend time to bond and listen, and to educate them on what AI is used for, I wouldn’t be too worried that AI will destroy our kids and humanity.

In a way, just like inventions like the Internet, software, and coding, if a kid understands the function of AI as a tool and can learn how to best leverage it, this kid will go out and succeed.

Every generation has its scapegoats for ruining its kids. That’s just an easy way to explain why things are bad. But there is always a group of people who make good use of inventions.

Let’s be in this group.
